At this point there are at least five progressive combinations of predictor sets that
could be explored for their predictive ability: original risk set alone, imaging predictors alone, risk and imaging predictors together, imaging predictors and interactions of imaging predictors, and risk, imaging predictors, and interactions of imaging predictors. We’ll consider modeling several of these sets of data in turn.

it is well known that logistic regression is a high-bias, low-variance model which has a tendency to yield lower predictivity than other low-bias, high-variance models. The predictive performance of logistic regression is also degraded by the inclusion of correlated, non-informative predictors. In order to find the most predictive logistic regression model, the most relevant predictors should be identified to find the best subset for predicting stroke risk.

> [!note] "Predictive performance is degraded by correlated, non-informative predictors"
> This refers to **multicollinearity**:
> - If two or more predictors are highly correlated, logistic regression has trouble estimating their individual effects.
> -  And if some predictors are **not informative** (don't actually help predict stroke), they just add noise.
>📉 The result: worse performance, especially in terms of generalizability to new data.

> [!note] 🧠 **"To find the most predictive logistic regression model..."**
> Avoid:
> - Uninformative noise variables
> - Redundant (highly correlated) variables
