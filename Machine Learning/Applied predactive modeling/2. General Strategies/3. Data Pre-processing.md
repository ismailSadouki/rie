The relationship between the predictor and the outcome is a second factor.
For example, if there were a seasonal component to these data, and it appears
that there is, then the numeric day of the year would be best. Also, if some
months showed higher success rates than others, then the encoding based on
the month is preferable.

# 1 Case Study: Cell Segmentation in High-Content Screening
Researchers often need to **measure physical or biological characteristics** of cells (like size, shape, or structure). Instead of measuring them by hand (which is impossible for thousands of cells), they use **automated imaging and analysis methods** — and one of them is called **high-content screening (HCS)**.
### ⚙️ Step-by-step explanation

1. **Sample preparation**
    
    - A biological sample (e.g., a slide of cells) is **dyed** with a chemical that attaches specifically to something in the cell.
        
    - Example:  
        If you want to study **cell nuclei**, use a dye that binds to **DNA** (since DNA is located in the nucleus).
        
2. **Fixation**
    
    - The cells are **“fixed”** — meaning they’re treated to preserve their structure so they don’t degrade or change during measurement.
        
    - Think of it as freezing the cells in a stable condition.
        
3. **Measurement with instruments**
    
    - Instruments like **confocal microscopes** shine light of specific **wavelengths** (colors) on the sample.
        
    - The **dye reflects or deflects** the light — different dyes respond differently to certain wavelengths.
        
    - Detectors then measure how much light is scattered or emitted — that’s a **quantitative signal**.
        
4. **Multiple characteristics**
    
    - If you want to study multiple features (like nucleus size, shape, and texture), you can use:
        
        - Multiple dyes (each binding to different parts of the cell)
            
        - Multiple light frequencies (so you can distinguish which dye is which)
            
5. **Image processing**
    
    - The microscope produces **digital images** that are then analyzed by **imaging software**.
        
    - The software extracts **numerical features** — for example:
        
        - Average nucleus area
            
        - Circularity
            
        - Intensity of fluorescence (light emission)
            
    
    These become **variables (predictors)** in a dataset used for predictive modeling.
    

---

![](https://i.imgur.com/3Y0QaMu.png)
Even though **high-content screening (HCS)** automates the process of measuring cells’ characteristics, it can sometimes produce **inaccurate or misleading measurements** because of **errors in image segmentation** — the step where software tries to separate one cell from another in an image.
### 🧩 **Key Terms Explained**

1. **Automated, high-throughput approach**
    
    - “High-throughput” means analyzing **a large number of samples quickly and automatically** using machines.
        
    - This is great for efficiency — but it can also **amplify small errors** across many samples.
        
2. **Segmentation**
    
    - Segmentation is the process of **identifying each individual cell** in an image and defining its **boundaries** (edges).
        
    - It’s like tracing around each cell so the computer knows:
        
        - “This is one cell.”
            
        - “This is its nucleus.”
            
        - “This is its shape and size."
**The problem (Hill et al., 2007)**

- The software had **difficulty segmenting some cells correctly.**
    
- Some cells were **“poorly segmented”** — meaning their boundaries were drawn incorrectly.
    
- As a result:
    
    - They appeared **smaller or misshaped**.
        
    - Some looked **damaged** when they were not.
        

This leads to **wrong data** (measurement error).

- **Figure 3.1 (in the book)**
    
    - The figure shows microscope images:
        
        - **Green outlines** = nucleus boundaries
            
        - **Blue outlines** = entire cell boundaries
            
    - Some cells are **accurately outlined**, others are not.
        
    - This visually shows how **segmentation errors** can distort the features extracted from the image.
        
- **Why it matters**
    
    - If the **study’s goal** is to measure things like:
        
        - Cell size
            
        - Cell shape
            
        - Cell count  
            Then **segmentation accuracy** directly affects the quality of your predictors (features).
            
    - Bad segmentation → bad data → bad model.
This example illustrates an important **data preprocessing principle**:

> “Garbage in, garbage out.”

If your measurement process (imaging, sensors, or software) introduces **systematic errors**, even a perfectly tuned machine learning model will produce **misleading conclusions**.

Before modeling, always:

- **Check data quality** (visual inspection, summary stats, outlier detection).
    
- **Validate the measurement instrument** (is it reliable?).
    
- **Clean or filter** suspicious observations (e.g., damaged or poorly segmented cells).

# 3.2 Data Transformations for Individual Predictors

#### Centering and Scaling
Scaling the data coerce the values to have a common standard deviation of one.
These manipulations are generally used to improve the numerical stability of some calculations. The only real downside to these transformations is a loss
of interpretability of the individual values since the data are no longer in the
original units.

### Transformations to Resolve Skewness
Another common reason for transformations is to remove distributional skew-
ness.An un-skewed distribution is one that is roughly symmetric. This means
that the probability of falling on either side of the distribution’s mean is
roughly equal.
If the **largest value ÷ smallest value** is **greater than 20**,  
then the data are likely to be **significantly skewed**.
When one value is **20× (or more) larger** than another, it means the data **spans a wide range** — some values are much bigger than others.
The ratio doesn’t _prove_ skewness mathematically, but it’s a **quick heuristic** before running formal statistics (like skewness coefficient or visualizing histograms).

Also, the skewness statistic can be used as a diagnostic. If the predictor
distribution is roughly symmetric, the skewness values will be close to zero.
As
the distribution becomes more right skewed, the skewness statistic becomes
larger. Similarly, as the distribution becomes more left skewed, the value
becomes negative.
![](https://i.imgur.com/gh3ae4S.png)


Replacing the data with the log, square root, or inverse may help to remove
the skew.
Alternatively, statistical methods can be used to empirically identify an
appropriate transformation. Box and Cox (1964) propose a family of trans-
formations3 that are indexed by a parameter, denoted as λ:
![](https://i.imgur.com/KBqOTvC.png)
In addition to the log transformation, this family can identify square trans-
formation (λ = 2), square root (λ = 0.5), inverse (λ = −1), and others
in-between. Using the training data, λ can be estimated. Box and Cox (1964)
show how to use maximum likelihood estimation to determine the transfor-
mation parameter. This procedure would be applied independently to each
predictor data that contain values greater than zero.

### Transformations to Resolve Outliers
With small sample sizes, apparent
outliers might be a result of a skewed distribution where there are not yet enough data to see the skewness. Also, the outlying data may be an indica-
tion of a special part of the population under study that is just starting to be
sampled.

There are several predictive models that are resistant to outliers. Tree-
based classiﬁcation models create splits of the training data and the predic-
tion equation is a set of logical statements such as “if predictor A is greater
than X, predict the class to be Y ,” so the outlier does not usually have
an exceptional inﬂuence on the model. Also, support vector machines for
classiﬁcation generally disregard a portion of the training set samples when
creating a prediction equation. The excluded samples may be far away from
the decision boundary and outside of the data mainstream.
> [!note]
> for SVMs:
>
>- The **decision boundary** is determined only by the **support vectors**, which lie **near the boundary between classes**.
    >
>- **Points far away** from the decision boundary (like extreme outliers) **don’t affect the boundary** at all, because they are **not support vectors**.
  >  
>
>Hence, SVMs **“disregard a portion of the training set”** — they effectively **ignore** those distant outliers when computing the separating line.

If a model is considered to be sensitive to outliers, one data transformation
that can minimize the problem is the <mark>spatial sign</mark>.
This
procedure projects the predictor values onto a multidimensional sphere. This
has the eﬀect of making all the samples the same distance from the center of
![](https://i.imgur.com/VeH3pUa.png)

Since the denominator is intended to measure the squared distance to the
center of the predictor’s distribution, it is important to center and scale the
predictor data prior to using this transformation. Note that, unlike centering
or scaling, this manipulation of the predictors transforms them as a group.
Removing predictor variables after applying the spatial sign transformation
may be problematic.

---
#### 🌍 2. The idea of the _spatial sign transformation_
The spatial sign transformation **projects all data points onto the surface of a sphere** in multidimensional space.

That means:

- Every point keeps its **direction** (the line from the center of the data to the point),
    
- But its **distance (length)** from the center becomes **the same for all points**.
    

So — all points end up at **the same distance from the center** — like stars on the surface of a globe.
![](https://i.imgur.com/weF6Tg1.png)
### ⚙️ 4. What happens geometrically

- Each sample (point) now lies **on the surface of a unit sphere** (distance = 1 from the center).
    
- This **removes magnitude differences** between samples.
    
- Only **directional information** remains — i.e., how each variable contributes _relative_ to others.
    

This is why it’s sometimes called a **“directional normalization.”**

### 🔄 5. Why you should center and scale first

The transformation divides by the distance from the **center** of the data.  
But the formula assumes that “center” is **(0, 0, …, 0)**.

So before applying it, you must:

1. **Center** each predictor (subtract its mean), and
    
2. **Scale** it (divide by its standard deviation).
    

Otherwise, the “center” of the data isn’t at the origin, and the transformation won’t behave correctly.

### 🚫 6. Why you shouldn’t remove predictors afterward

This transformation operates on **all predictors together** — it depends on the _entire vector_ for each sample.

If you remove one variable **after** applying the transformation, the geometry changes:

- The point is no longer guaranteed to lie on the unit sphere.
    
- You break the internal consistency of the transformation.
    

So, you must decide which predictors to keep **before** applying the spatial sign.

### ✅ 7. Why it’s useful

The spatial sign:

- **Reduces the influence of outliers**, because all samples have the same length (extreme points can’t dominate).
    
- Preserves **directional structure**, so relationships between predictors remain meaningful.
    
- Is often used before robust multivariate methods (e.g., robust PCA, robust regression)

So it’s applied **before**:

- Model fitting (e.g., regression, SVM, PCA, etc.)
    
- Distance-based algorithms (e.g., k-NN, clustering)
    
- Any step that depends on the relative scale or position of samples in feature space

### ⚠️ **1. Loss of magnitude information**

The biggest drawback:

> When you project data onto a unit sphere, you completely **remove magnitude (length)** information.

- Every sample ends up at the **same distance** from the center (radius = 1).
    
- So, if the **absolute size** of a measurement carries useful information (e.g., intensity, volume, or concentration), that information is **lost**.
    

✅ You keep _direction_ (relative pattern between features).  
❌ You lose _how large_ each observation really is.

### ⚠️ **2. Can distort relationships between predictors**

Because the transformation works on **all predictors together**, it changes how variables relate to one another.

- It’s a **nonlinear transformation** of the entire feature vector.
    
- So correlations between variables may change.
    
- If you later try to remove or add predictors, it breaks the consistency of the transformation.
    

That’s why the book warns:

> “Removing predictor variables after applying the spatial sign transformation may be problematic.”

### ⚠️ **4. Not suitable for all data types**

- Works only for **numeric, continuous** predictors.
    
- Not useful for **categorical variables** or **binary features**.
    
- Not ideal for data where **distance magnitudes** are essential (e.g., physical measurements, economics).

### ⚠️ **6. Can hurt performance if outliers aren’t a problem**

If your dataset **doesn’t contain significant outliers**, applying this transformation might:

- Overcomplicate preprocessing,
    
- Reduce signal (because you remove meaningful magnitude variation),
    
- And **worsen model performance**.

#### Data Reduction and Feature Extraction
PCA is a commonly used data reduction technique (Abdi and Williams
2010). This method seeks to ﬁnd linear combinations of the predictors, known
as principal components (PCs), which capture the most possible variance. The
ﬁrst PC is deﬁned as the linear combination of the predictors that captures
the most variability of all possible linear combinations. Then, subsequent PCs
are derived such that these linear combinations capture the most remaining
variability while also being uncorrelated with all previous PCs. Mathemati-
cally, the jth PC can be written as:
![](https://i.imgur.com/nGsg5d9.png)
P is the number of predictors. The coeﬃcients aj1 , aj2 ,. . . , ajP are called com-
ponent weights and help us understand which predictors are most important
to each PC.

The primary advantage of PCA, and the reason that it has retained its
popularity as a data reduction method, is that it creates components that are
uncorrelated.
As mentioned earlier in this chapter, some predictive models
prefer predictors to be uncorrelated (or at least low correlation) in order
to ﬁnd solutions and to improve the model’s numerical stability. PCA pre-
processing creates new predictors with desirable characteristics for these kinds
of models.
> [!note] You can **apply PCA first as a preprocessing step**, and then **fit your predictive model** (like linear regression, logistic regression, or LDA) on the _principal components_ instead of the original correlated variables.

practitioners must under-
stand that PCA seeks predictor-set variation without regard to any further
understanding of the predictors (i.e., measurement scales or distributions) or to knowledge of the modeling objectives (i.e., response variable). Hence,
without proper guidance, PCA can generate components that summarize
characteristics of the data that are irrelevant to the underlying structure of
the data and also to the ultimate modeling objective.
Because PCA seeks linear combinations of predictors that maximize
variability, it will naturally ﬁrst be drawn to summarizing predictors that
have more variation. If the original predictors are on measurement scales that
diﬀer in orders of magnitude [consider demographic predictors such as income
level (in dollars) and height (in feet)], then the ﬁrst few components will fo-
cus on summarizing the higher magnitude predictors (e.g., income), while
latter components will summarize lower variance predictors (e.g., height).
This means that the PC weights will be larger for the higher variability pre-
dictors on the ﬁrst few components. In addition, it means that PCA will be
focusing its eﬀorts on identifying the data structure based on measurement
scales rather than based on the important relationships within the data for
the current problem.

For most data sets, predictors are on diﬀerent scales. In addition, predic-
tors may have skewed distributions. Hence, to help PCA avoid summarizing
distributional diﬀerences and predictor scale information, it is best to ﬁrst
transform skewed predictors (Sect. 3.2) and then center and scale the predic-
tors prior to performing PCA. Centering and scaling enables PCA to ﬁnd the
underlying relationships in the data without being inﬂuenced by the original
measurement scales.


If the predictive relationship
between the predictors and response is not connected to the predictors’ vari-
ability, then the derived PCs will not provide a suitable relationship with the
response. In this case, a supervised technique, like PLS (Sects. 6.3 and 12.4),
will derive components while simultaneously considering the corresponding
response.
Once we have decided on the appropriate transformations of the predictor
variables, we can then apply PCA. For data sets with many predictor vari-
ables, we must decide how many components to retain. A heuristic approach
for determining the number of components to retain is to create a scree plot,
which contains the ordered component number (x-axis) and the amount of
summarized variability (y-axis) (Fig. 3.6).
![](https://i.imgur.com/9SyiZGd.png)
he optimal number of components can be determined by cross-validation.

Visually examining the principal components is a critical step for assessing
data quality and gaining intuition for the problem. To do this, the ﬁrst few
principal components can be plotted against each other and the plot symbols
can be colored by relevant characteristics, such as the class labels. If PCA
has captured a suﬃcient amount of information in the data, this type of plot
can demonstrate clusters of samples or outliers that may prompt a closer ex-
amination of the individual data points. For classiﬁcation problems, the PCA
plot can show potential separation of classes (if there is a separation).
This
can set the initial expectations of the modeler; if there is little clustering of
the classes, the plot of the principal component values will show a signiﬁcant
overlap of the points for each class. Care should be taken when plotting the
components; the scale of the components tend to become smaller as they
account for less and less variation in the data.
If the axes are displayed on separate scales, there is
the potential to over-interpret any patterns that might be seen for compo-
nents that account for small amounts of variation. See Geladi, Manley, and
Lestander (2003) for other examples of this issue.

#### Dealing with Missing Values
It is important to understand why the values are missing. First and fore-
most, it is important to know if the pattern of missing data is related to
the outcome. This is called “informative missingness” since the missing data
pattern is instructional on its own. Informative missingness can induce sig-
niﬁcant bias in the model.
In the Netﬂix Prize machine learning
competition to predict which movies people will like based on their previous
ratings, the “Napoleon Dynamite Eﬀect” confounded many of the contestants
because people who did rate the movie Napoleon Dynamite either loved or
hated it.
### 🔹 What this teaches us

The “Napoleon Dynamite Effect” illustrates that:

1. **Some data points are inherently unpredictable** — they add randomness that models can’t capture.
    
2. **Outliers in user behavior** (or in any predictive setting) can reduce model performance.
    
3. Sometimes, it’s better to **treat such cases separately** or **down-weight them** during training.

### 🔹 Analogy beyond movies

This happens in many datasets:

- In finance: a customer whose behavior doesn’t match any segment
    
- In health: a patient with rare or contradictory symptoms
    
- In marketing: a product everyone either loves or hates
    

These create “high-variance” points that confuse predictive algorithms.


Imputation has been extensively studied in the statistical literature, but
in the context of generating correct hypothesis testing procedures in the pres-
ence of missing data. This is a separate problem; for predictive models we
are concerned about accuracy of the predictions rather than making valid
inferences. There is a small literature on imputation for predictive models.
Saar-Tsechansky and Provost (2007b) examine the issue of missing values
and delve into how speciﬁc models deal with the issue. Jerez et al. (2010)
also look at a wide variety of imputation methods for a speciﬁc data set.

As previously mentioned, imputation is just another layer of modeling
where we try to estimate values of the predictor variables based on other
predictor variables. The most relevant scheme for accomplishing this is to
use the training set to built an imputation model for each predictor in the
data set. Prior to model training or the prediction of new samples, missing
values are ﬁlled in using imputation. Note that this extra layer of models adds
uncertainty. If we are using resampling to select tuning parameter values or
to estimate performance, the imputation should be incorporated within the
resampling. This will increase the computational time for building models,
but it will also provide honest estimates of model performance.
If the number of predictors aﬀected by missing values is small, an ex-
ploratory analysis of the relationships between the predictors is a good idea.
For example, visualizations or methods like PCA can be used to determine if
there are strong relationships between the predictors. If a variable with miss-
ing values is highly correlated with another predictor that has few missing
values, a focused model can often be eﬀective for imputation (see the example
below).

One popular technique for imputation is a K-nearest neighbor model.
A new sample is imputed by ﬁnding the samples in the training set “closest”
to it and averages these nearby points to ﬁll in the value.Troyanskaya et al.
(2001) examine this approach for high-dimensional data with small sample
sizes. One advantage of this approach is that the imputed data are conﬁned
to be within the range of the training set values. One disadvantage is that the
entire training set is required every time a missing value needs to be imputed.
Also, the number of neighbors is a tuning parameter, as is the method for determining “closeness” of two points. However, Troyanskaya et al. (2001) found
the nearest neighbor approach to be fairly robust to the tuning parameters,
as well as the amount of missing data.


### Removing Predictors
First, fewer predictors means decreased computational time and complexity.
Second, if two predictors are highly correlated, this implies that they are measuring the same underlying information. Removing one should not com-
promise the performance of the model and might lead to a more parsimonious
and interpretable model. Third, some models can be crippled by predictors
with degenerate distributions. In these cases, there can be a signiﬁcant im-
provement in model performance and/or stability without the problematic
variables.
> [!note] Some predictors (input variables) can have **degenerate distributions** — meaning their values carry **little or no useful information** for the model, or they can even **destabilize** it.
> A _degenerate_ predictor is one with **almost no variation** across samples.
> If all (or nearly all) observations have the same value, the variable provides **no signal** — it cannot help predict anything.

Consider a predictor variable that has a single unique value; we refer to
this type of data as a zero variance predictor. a model such as linear regression
would ﬁnd these data problematic and is likely to cause an error in the com-
putations. In either case, these data have no information and can easily be
discarded. Similarly, some predictors might have only a handful of unique
values that occur with very low frequencies.
a rule of thumb for detecting near-zero variance predictors is:
• The fraction of unique values over the sample size is low (say 10 %).
• The ratio of the frequency of the most prevalent value to the frequency of
the second most prevalent value is large (say around 20).
If both of these criteria are true and the model in question is susceptible to
this type of predictor, it may be advantageous to remove the variable from
the model.
### Between-Predictor Correlations
Collinearity is the technical term for the situation where a pair of pre-
dictor variables have a substantial correlation with each other. It is also
possible to have relationships between multiple predictors at once (called
multicollinearity).

techniques such as PCA can be used to characterize the magnitude of the

In general, there are good reasons to avoid data with highly correlated
predictors. First, redundant predictors frequently add more complexity to the
model than information they provide to the model. In situations where ob-
taining the predictor data is costly (either in time or money), fewer variables
is obviously better. While this argument is mostly philosophical, there are
mathematical disadvantages to having correlated predictor data. Using highly
correlated predictors in techniques like linear regression can result in highly
unstable models, numerical errors, and degraded predictive performance.
Classical regression analysis has several tools to diagnose multicollinearity
for linear regression. Since collinear predictors can impact the variance of
parameter estimates in this model, a statistic called the <mark>variance inﬂation
factor (VIF)</mark> can be used to identify predictors that are impacted (Myers
1994).
Beyond linear regression, this method may be inadequate for several
reasons: it was developed for linear models, it requires more samples than
predictor variables, and, while it does identify collinear predictors, it does
not determine which should be removed to resolve the problem.

to remove the minimum number of predictors to ensure that all pairwise
correlations are below a certain threshold. While this method only identify

the performance of some models.

the predictors and the outcome more complex. Additionally, since signal ex-

resulting surrogate predictors have any relationship with the outcome.


### Adding Predictors
When a predictor is categorical, such as gender or race, it is common to
decompose the predictor into a set of more speciﬁc variables.
Many of the models described in this text automatically generate highly
complex, nonlinear relationships between the predictors and the outcome.
More simplistic models do not unless the user manually speciﬁes which pre-
dictors should be nonlinear and in what way. For example, logistic regression
is a well-known classiﬁcation model that, by default, generates linear classi-
ﬁcation boundaries. Figure 3.11 shows another illustrative example with two

Additionally, Forina et al. (2009) demonstrate one technique for augment-
ing the prediction data with addition of complex combinations of the data.
For classiﬁcation models, they calculate the “class centroids,” which are the
centers of the predictor data for each class. Then for each predictor, the dis-
tance to each class centroid can be calculated and these distances can be
added to the model.

### 🔹 Why it helps:

These distance features capture **nonlinear relationships** and **class separability** that might not be clear from the raw predictors.  
For example:

- Points close to a particular class centroid are more likely to belong to that class.
    
- The distances summarize the geometry of the data in a simple but powerful way.

### 🔹 Intuition:

It’s like giving your model a “sense of proximity” to each class region in the feature space — a geometric shortcut that can make classification easier for models like logistic regression, kNN, or tree-based methods.


### Binning Predictors
While there are recommended techniques for pre-processing data, there are
also methods to avoid. One common approach to simplifying a data set is
to take a numeric predictor and pre-categorize or “bin” it into two or more
groups prior to data analysis.


---

### 🔹 Why this is **a bad idea (usually)**

1. **Loss of information:**  
    You throw away the fine-grained numeric variation in the data.  
    Example: ages 19 and 34 become “adult”, but they might have very different effects.
    
2. **Artificial cutpoints:**  
    The bin boundaries (like 18 or 35) are arbitrary and can create **false discontinuities** in your data.
    
3. **Reduced model performance:**  
    Many models (like linear regression, tree-based models, or neural nets) can naturally handle numeric predictors.  
    Turning them into categories reduces statistical power and predictive accuracy.
    
4. **Harder to generalize:**  
    When new unseen values fall into unexpected ranges (e.g., age = 90), they might not fit cleanly into your bins.

### 🔹 When binning _can_ be acceptable

Binning can be useful **only** in a few cases:

- For interpretability (like credit risk scoring: “income below $20k = high risk”).
    
- When a relationship is **strongly nonlinear** and difficult to model otherwise.
    
- When a domain expert defines meaningful bins (e.g., medical ranges for blood pressure).

---


Note that the argument here is related to the manual categorization of
predictors prior to model building. There are several models, such as clas-
siﬁcation/regression trees and multivariate adaptive regression splines, that
estimate cut points in the process of model building. The diﬀerence between
these methodologies and manual binning is that the models use all the predic-
tors to derive bins based on a single objective (such as maximizing accuracy).
They evaluate many variables simultaneously and are usually based on sta-
tistically sound methodologies.
