A few researchers from UC Berkeley think so too. In their paper presented at NIPS 2019, Roelofs, Fridovich-Keil et al. study in detail a few thousand Kaggle competitions to gain insight into the public-private leaderboard dynamics in Kaggle competitions. Although they focus on a limited subset of competitions (120, above a certain number of participants, focused on binary classification), they obtained some interesting findings:
- There is little adaptive overfitting; in other words, public standings usually do hold in the unveiled private leaderboard.
- Most shake-ups are due to random fluctuations and overcrowded rankings where competitors are too near to each other, and any slight change in the performance in the private test sets causes major changes in the rankings.
- Shake-ups happen when the training set is very small or the training data is not independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.).
> [!note] ### What are "Shake-ups"?
>
A **shake-up** refers to sudden and irregular fluctuations in the training process — for example, when accuracy or loss changes unpredictably between epochs, or validation performance jumps up and down instead of stabilizing.
> Shake-ups happen when the model doesn’t get a stable and representative signal from the data. This usually occurs with **small datasets** or when the **i.i.d. assumption is violated**, making training unstable and noisy.

machine learning. Proceedings of the 33rd International Conference on Neural In-

nips.cc/paper/2019/file/ee39e503b6bedf0c98c388b7e8589aca-Paper.pdf.


In our long experience of Kaggle competitions, however, we have seen quite a lot of problems with adaptive overfitting since the beginning. For instance, you can read Greg Park’s analysis of one of the first competitions we ever took part in: http://gregpark.io/blog/Kaggle-Psychopathy-Postmortem/. Since this is quite a common and persistent problem for many Kagglers, we suggest a strategy that is a bit more sophisticated than simply following what happens on the public leaderboard:
- Always build reliable cross-validation systems for local scoring.
- Always try to control non-i.i.d distributions using the best validation scheme dictated by the situation. Unless clearly stated in the description of the competition, it is not an easy task to spot non-i.i.d. distributions, but you can get hints from discussion or by experimenting using stratified validation schemes (when stratifying according to a certain feature, the results improve decisively, for instance).
> [!note] ### **ow to _spot_ non-i.i.d. issues**
>
It’s not always obvious, but you can get **hints** by:
>
>- Reading competition **forums/discussions** (other participants might notice distribution shifts).
  >  
>- Experimenting with **different validation schemes**:
  >  >
  >- If performance changes drastically when you stratify by some feature (say, region, product category, etc.), that’s a sign the data distribution is not i.i.d.

- Correlate local scoring with the public leaderboard in order to figure out whether or not they go in the same direction.
> [!note] (i.e., when your local score improves, your leaderboard score also improves), it means your validation scheme is **reliable**.
> ### **Example**
>
>1. You build model A →
 >   
>- Local validation = 0.75 AUC
  >   
>- Public leaderboard = 0.73 AUC
>    
>1. You improve feature engineering → model B →
 >   - Local validation = 0.78 AUC
>     
>- Public leaderboard = 0.76 AUC
>   
Since both improved, your **local validation correlates with leaderboard performance** → good validation scheme. ✅

- Test using adversarial validation, revealing whether or not the test distribution is similar to the training data.
- Make your solutions more robust using ensembling, especially if you are working with small datasets.

> [!note] ### **Adaptive Overfitting (a.k.a. _adaptive overfitting bias_)**
>
It happens when you **tune your model or training process too many times based on feedback from the validation/test set**.
> In other words, you’re not just overfitting to the training data — you start overfitting to the **evaluation data** (the set you’re supposed to use only for measuring generalization).
