Since this is a trial-and-error process, as you try to find the best validation strategy for the competition, you can systematically apply the following two consistency checks in order to figure out if you are on the right path:
1. First, you have to check if your local tests are consistent, that is, that the single cross-validation fold errors are not so different from each other or, when you opt for a simple train-test split, that the same results are reproducible using different train-test splits.
2. Then, you have to check if your local validation error is consistent with the results on the public leaderboard.

---
### **Why trial-and-error?**

In competitions, you often don’t know the true test set distribution.

- If your validation strategy doesn’t match it, your local scores can be misleading.
    
- So you try different splitting/validation schemes (time-based, stratified, group k-fold, etc.) until you find one that makes sense.
    

But how do you know if you’re on the right track? → You apply **consistency checks**
### **1. Consistency within local validation**

- In **cross-validation**, each fold should give a similar error.
    
    - If one fold gives AUC 0.90 but another gives 0.70, that’s a red flag — your data distribution may not be stable or representative.
        
- In a **train-test split**, if you repeat the split multiple times, your results should be reproducible.
    
    - If performance jumps around wildly depending on the split, your validation is not reliable.
        

✅ The goal: make sure your local evaluation method itself is **internally stable**.

### **2. Consistency with the public leaderboard**

- Next, you compare your **local validation error** with the **public leaderboard score**.
    
- If they **move in the same direction** (when your local score improves, leaderboard score also improves), then your validation scheme is probably aligned with the hidden test distribution.
    
- If they move in **opposite directions** (local score goes up, leaderboard score goes down), it means your local validation is **misleading**, and you need to rethink your split strategy.
    

✅ The goal: make sure your local validation is **externally reliable**, i.e., predictive of real leaderboard performance.

### ✅ **In short**

- **Step 1:** Check **internal consistency** → your local validation scores should be stable across folds or splits.
    
- **Step 2:** Check **external consistency** → your local validation should correlate with the public leaderboard.
    

If both checks hold, you can trust your validation scheme. If not, you’re probably validating on a biased or non-representative sample.



---

If you’re failing the first check, you have a few options depending on the following possible origins of the problem:
- You don’t have much training data
- The data is too diverse and every training partition is very different from every other (for instance, if you have too many high cardinality features, that is, features with too many levels – like zip codes – or if you have multivariate outliers)
In both cases, the point is that you lack data with respect to the model you want to implement.

Even when the problem just appears to be that the data is too diverse, plotting learning curves will make it evident to you that your model needs more data.

In this case, unless you find out that moving to a simpler algorithm works on the evaluation metric (in which case trading variance for bias may worsen your model’s performance, but not always), your best choice is to use an extensive validation approach. This can be implemented by:
- Using larger k values (thus approaching LOO where k = n). Your validation results will be less about the capability of your model to perform on unseen data, but by using larger training portions, you will have the advantage of more stable evaluations.
- Averaging the results of multiple k-fold validations (based on different data partitions picked by different random seed initializations).
- Using repetitive bootstrapping.
Keep in mind that when you find unstable local validation results, you won’t be the only one to suffer from the problem. Usually, this is a common problem due to the data’s origin and characteristics. By keeping tuned in to the discussion forums, you may get hints at possible solutions. For instance, a good solution for high cardinality features is target encoding; stratification can help with outliers; and so on.


<mark>The situation is different when you’ve passed the first check but failed the second; your local cross-validation is consistent but you find that it doesn’t hold on the leaderboard. In order to realize this problem exists, you have to keep diligent note of all your experiments, validation test types, random seeds used, and leaderboard results if you submitted the resulting predictions. In this way, you can draw a simple scatterplot and try fitting a linear regression or, even simpler, compute a correlation between your local results and the associated public leaderboard scores. It costs some time and patience to annotate and analyze all of these, but it is the most important meta-analysis of your competition performances that you can keep track of.</mark>


When the mismatch is because your validation score is systematically lower or higher than the leaderboard score, you actually have a strong signal that something is missing from your validation strategy, but this problem does not prevent you from improving your model. In fact, you can keep on working on your model and expect improvements to be reflected on the leaderboard, though not in a proportional way. However, systematic differences are always a red flag, implying something is different between what you are doing and what the organizers have arranged for testing the model.

---
### **1. What is happening?**

- Sometimes, your **local validation score** (on your training/validation split) is **consistently higher or lower** than your **public leaderboard score**.
    
- This is called a **systematic mismatch**.
    

Example:

- Your CV gives **0.85 AUC** every time.
    
- Public leaderboard shows **0.80 AUC** consistently.
### **2. What does it mean?**

- This systematic difference is a **signal** that your validation split does **not perfectly reflect the true test distribution**.
    
- Something is different between your validation set and the organizer’s test set:
    
    - Maybe your validation set is too easy or too hard.
        
    - Maybe there are features or distributions in the test set that aren’t in your training/validation set.
### **3. Does this stop you from improving the model?**

- **No.** You can still improve your model.
    
- Your improvements on your local validation set are **likely to be reflected on the leaderboard**, even if the scores don’t match perfectly.
    
- But the **improvement might not be proportional**.
    
    - Example: local AUC improves by 0.02 → leaderboard might improve only 0.01.

### **4. Key takeaway**

- **Systematic mismatch ≠ fatal problem**, but it is a **red flag**.
    
- It signals that your validation strategy is not perfectly aligned with the real test conditions.
    
- You should still iterate on the model, but **always be aware** that your local metrics may not tell the full story about generalization.
---




An even worse scenario occurs when your local cross-validation scores do not correlate at all with the leaderboard feedback. This is really a red flag. When you realize this is the case, you should immediately run a series of tests and investigations in order to figure out why, because, regardless of whether it is a common problem or not, the situation poses a serious threat to your final rankings. There are a few possibilities in such a scenario:
- You figure out that the test set is drawn from a different distribution to the training set. The adversarial validation test (that we will discuss in the next section) is the method that can enlighten you in such a situation.
- The data is non-i.i.d. but this is not explicit. For instance, in The Nature Conservancy Fisheries Monitoring competition (https://www.kaggle.com/c/the-nature-conservancy-fisheries-monitoring), images in the training set were taken from similar situations (fishing boats). You had to figure out by yourself how to arrange them in order to avoid the model learning to identify the target rather than the context of the images (see, for instance, this work by Anokas: https://www.kaggle.com/anokas/finding-boatids).
- The multivariate distribution of the features is the same, but some groups are distributed differently in the test set. If you can figure out the differences, you can set your training set and your validation accordingly and gain an edge. You need to probe the public leaderboard to work this out.

---
### **1. The setup**

- You have a dataset with multiple features (multivariate).
    
- **Multivariate distribution is the same** → overall, the features have similar ranges, means, correlations, etc., in training and test.
    
- **But some groups are distributed differently** → subsets of the data behave differently in the test set compared to training.
    

Example:

- Feature = “region”
    
- Training set: 50% Region A, 50% Region B
    
- Test set: 30% Region A, 70% Region B
    
- Even if the overall feature distributions (means, variances) look similar, the **composition of groups** is different.
### **2. Why it matters**

- If your validation split doesn’t account for these group differences, your local validation may **not reflect leaderboard performance**.
    
- Your model could perform well locally but worse on the leaderboard because it **overfits to the group proportions in your training/validation set**.

### **3. How to fix / gain an edge**

- **Identify the groups** that differ between training and test.
    
    - E.g., categories, regions, time periods, user segments.
        
- **Adjust your validation set** to mimic the test set distribution:
    
    - Stratify validation folds according to the groups.
        
    - Oversample or undersample groups in training to better match test proportions.
        
- By doing this, your local validation becomes **more predictive of leaderboard performance**, giving you a competitive edge.

### **4. Role of the public leaderboard**

- You may not know the exact group differences from the organizers.
    
- By **probing the leaderboard** (testing different validation splits and features, observing score changes), you can **reverse-engineer hints** about the test set distribution.
    
- This is common in competitions: careful experiments with local validation + leaderboard feedback reveal hidden patterns.
✅ **In short:**

- Overall feature distribution = similar → good.
    
- Group distribution = different → problem.
    
- Adjust validation to reflect test group proportions → better leaderboard alignment.
    
- Use leaderboard as a probe to infer test set structure.

---





- The test data is drifted or trended, which is usually the case in time series predictions. Again, you need to probe the public leaderboard to get some insight about some possible post-processing that could help your score, for instance, applying a multiplier to your predictions, thus mimicking a decreasing or increasing trend in the test data.
As we’ve discussed before, probing the leaderboard is the act of making specifically devised sub-missions in order to get insights about the composition of the public test set. It works particularly well if the private test set is similar to the public one. There are no general methods for probing, so you have to devise a probing methodology according to the type of competition and problem.

For instance, in the paper Climbing the Kaggle Leaderboard by Exploiting the Log-Loss Oracle (https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/1707.01825), Jacob explains how to get fourth position in a competition without even downloading the training data.


With regard to regression problems, in the recent 30 Days of ML organized by Kaggle, Hung Khoi explained how probing the leaderboard helped him to understand the differences in the mean and standard deviation of the target column between the training dataset and the public test data (see: https://www.kaggle.com/c/30-days-of-ml/discussion/269541).
