Understanding Word Representations

- Traditional word representation methods, like one-hot vectors, treat words as isolated entities, making it difficult for algorithms to generalize relationships between words.
- For example, the one-hot representation fails to recognize that "apple" and "orange" are more similar than "king" and "orange."

Introduction to Word Embeddings

- Word embeddings allow for a more nuanced representation of words by using feature vectors that capture various attributes (e.g., gender, royalty, age).
- This representation enhances the algorithm's ability to generalize, as similar words will have closer feature vectors.

Visualization and Applications

- Techniques like t-SNE can visualize high-dimensional word embeddings in lower dimensions, revealing clusters of related words.
- Word embeddings are crucial in NLP, enabling algorithms to understand and process language more effectively.

# Using Word Embeddings
![](https://i.imgur.com/OHmSQ6L.png)
Understanding Word Embeddings

- Word embeddings are dense vector representations of words that capture semantic relationships.
- They allow models to generalize better by recognizing similarities between words, such as "orange" and "durian."

Transfer Learning in NLP

- Transfer learning involves using knowledge gained from large, unlabeled text corpora to improve performance on tasks with smaller labeled datasets.
- For example, if a model learns that "orange" and "apple" are fruits, it can infer that "durian" is also likely a fruit, aiding in named entity recognition.

Applications and Limitations

- Word embeddings are beneficial for various NLP tasks like named entity recognition, text summarization, and co-reference resolution.
- They are less effective in tasks like language modeling and machine translation when ample data is available for those specific tasks.

In summary, word embeddings enhance the ability of NLP models to learn from limited data by leveraging broader semantic knowledge acquired from extensive text sources.

# Properties of Word Embeddings

Understanding Word Embeddings

- Word embeddings are vector representations of words that capture semantic relationships.
- They can be used to solve analogy problems, such as "man is to woman as king is to what?"

Analogy Reasoning with Vectors

- The difference between vectors representing "man" and "woman" is similar to the difference between "king" and "queen."
- An algorithm can compute these differences to find analogous words by maximizing similarity.

Cosine Similarity

- Cosine similarity is a common method to measure the similarity between two vectors.
- It calculates the cosine of the angle between vectors, providing a value between -1 and 1, where 1 indicates identical direction and 0 indicates orthogonality.

Generalization of Analogy Relationships

- Word embeddings can learn various analogy relationships, such as gender differences and capital-city relationships.
- This capability allows them to identify patterns in large text corpora without explicit programming.

# Learning Word Embeddings
Neural Language Model

- A neural network can be trained to predict the next word in a sequence, using input words and their corresponding one-hot vectors.
- The model learns word embeddings by optimizing a matrix of parameters through gradient descent, aiming to predict the next word based on a fixed context of previous words.

Contextual Learning Approaches

- Different contexts can be used for learning embeddings, such as the last few words or a combination of words from both sides of the target word.
- The choice of context affects the learning problem, with simpler contexts (like one word) still yielding meaningful embeddings.

Skip-Gram Model

- The Skip-Gram model is introduced as a simpler algorithm that predicts a target word based on a single context word.
- Researchers found that using various contexts can lead to effective word embeddings, emphasizing the flexibility in designing learning problems for embedding generation.

# Word2Vec
Skip-Gram Model Overview

- The skip-gram model predicts target words based on a given context word, using a random selection of words within a specified window.
- The goal is to create a supervised learning problem that helps in learning good word embeddings rather than just achieving high accuracy on the prediction task.

Model Details

- The model uses a vocabulary of words and represents context words as one-hot vectors, which are then transformed into embedding vectors.
- A softmax unit is employed to output probabilities for the target words, with a loss function based on negative log likelihood.

Challenges and Solutions

- A primary challenge is the computational speed due to the need to sum over the entire vocabulary for softmax calculations.
- Hierarchical softmax classifiers are introduced as a solution, allowing for faster classification by organizing words in a tree structure, reducing the number of computations needed.

Sampling Context

- The context word sampling can be biased towards less frequent words to ensure a balanced training set, preventing common words from dominating the learning process.

In summary, the skip-gram model is a foundational technique in natural language processing for generating word embeddings, with considerations for computational efficiency and balanced training.

# Negative Sampling

The content focuses on the concept of negative sampling in the context of word embeddings, specifically as an efficient alternative to the Skip-Gram model.

Negative Sampling Overview

- Introduces a new supervised learning problem where the goal is to predict if a pair of words (context and target) is a valid context-target pair.
- Positive examples are generated by sampling context words and their associated target words, while negative examples are created by randomly selecting words from the dictionary.

Training Set Generation

- The training set consists of positive examples labeled as 1 and negative examples labeled as 0.
- The number of negative examples (k) can vary based on dataset size, with recommendations for k being 5-20 for smaller datasets and 2-5 for larger ones.

Logistic Regression Model

- A logistic regression model is defined to estimate the probability of a context-target pair being valid.
- The model uses a k:1 ratio of negative to positive examples, allowing for efficient training of multiple binary classifiers instead of a single large softmax classifier.

Negative Example Selection

- Discusses strategies for selecting negative examples, suggesting a balance between empirical frequency and uniform distribution.
- A heuristic approach is recommended, sampling words based on their frequency raised to the power of 3/4, which has shown effective results in practice.

Conclusion

- Negative sampling significantly reduces computational costs while learning word vectors, making it a practical approach in natural language processing tasks.

# GloVe Word Vectors
GloVe Algorithm Overview

- Developed by Jeffrey Pennington, Richard Socher, and Chris Manning, GloVe stands for Global Vectors for Word Representation.
- It counts how often words appear in the context of each other, using a matrix X_ij to represent these co-occurrences.

Optimization Process

- The algorithm minimizes the difference between the predicted and actual co-occurrence counts using gradient descent.
- A weighting function is introduced to handle frequent and infrequent words, ensuring meaningful computation for both.

Symmetry in Parameters

- The parameters theta and e in the GloVe model are symmetric, allowing for flexibility in their roles during training.
- The final word embeddings are derived from the average of the trained parameters.

Interpretability of Embeddings

- Individual components of the embeddings may not be easily interpretable, as they can represent a combination of various features.
- Despite this, the learned embeddings can still effectively capture relationships between words, such as analogies.

# Debiasing Word Embeddings
Understanding Bias in Word Embeddings

- Bias in this context refers to undesirable stereotypes related to gender, ethnicity, and sexual orientation.
- Word embeddings can reflect societal biases, leading to problematic associations, such as linking "woman" with "homemaker" instead of "computer programmer."

Methods to Reduce Bias

- The process involves identifying the direction of bias (e.g., gender) in the word embeddings.
- A neutralization step projects non-definitional words (like "doctor") to eliminate their bias components.

Equalization of Word Pairs

- The final step ensures that pairs of words with intrinsic gender differences (like "grandmother" and "grandfather") are equidistant from gender-neutral terms.
- A classifier is used to determine which words should retain their gender specificity and which should be neutralized.

Importance of Addressing Bias

- Reducing bias in algorithms is crucial as they influence significant societal decisions, from job applications to criminal justice.
- Ongoing research aims to improve methods for bias reduction in AI systems.
