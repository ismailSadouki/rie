In (2.2), we defined πi to be the probability that unit i is included in the sample. In
probability sampling, these inclusion probabilities are used to calculate point estimates such as t̂ and ȳ. Define the sampling weight, sometimes called the design weight, to be the reciprocal of the inclusion probability:
![](https://i.imgur.com/x1P1nSA.png)
The sampling weight of sampled unit i can be interpreted as the number of population units represented by unit i.
In an SRS, each unit has inclusion probability πi = n/N ; consequently, all sampling
weights are the same with wi = 1/πi = N/n. We can thus think of every unit in the sample as representing the same number of units, N/n, in the population—itself plus N/n − 1 of the unsampled units. We call such a sample, in which every unit has the same sampling weight, a self-weighting sample.

Recall from Chapter 1 that a sample is representative if it can be used to “reconstruct” what the population looks like, and provide an accurate measure of how close statistics calculated from the reconstructed population are likely to be to statistics calculated from the true, but unknown, population. Since we do not know the original population, we estimate it, conceptually, from the sample by creating wi copies of unit i, for each unit in S. In a self-weighting sample such as an SRS, we can think of the estimated population as consisting of N/n copies of the sample.

Of course, we do not create a physical data file with N entries. Instead, we use the
sampling weights directly for all calculations. The population size is estimated by the sum of the weights for the sample,
