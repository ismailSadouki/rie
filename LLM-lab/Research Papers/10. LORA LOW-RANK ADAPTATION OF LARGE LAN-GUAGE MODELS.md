

**Fine-tuning GPT-3 175B requires updating 175 billion parameters. LoRA does it by updating as few as 0.01% of them — and matches the performance.**

That's the core claim of the LoRA paper (Hu et al., 2021), and it holds up.

The key insight is a hypothesis about how pretrained weights change during fine-tuning: the updates don't actually need to live in the full high-dimensional weight space. They can be approximated by a much lower-rank matrix. So instead of modifying the original weights directly, LoRA freezes them and injects two small trainable matrices — A and B — whose product represents the update. Rank 8 instead of 4096. The original model is untouched.

The practical consequences are significant. No additional inference latency, since the adapter matrices can be merged back into the original weights after training. Multiple task-specific adapters can share one base model, each costing a fraction of the storage. Fine-tuning a 175B model becomes feasible on a single GPU setup that would otherwise be completely out of reach.

The honest limitation: LoRA works well when the task is related to what the base model already knows. For genuinely out-of-distribution tasks or dramatic style shifts, full fine-tuning still wins. And the original paper's choice of which layers to apply LoRA to (mostly attention) has since been questioned — later work shows broader application across layers often helps.

**Why it matters now:** LoRA is everywhere. It's the default fine-tuning method in HuggingFace PEFT, LLaMA-Factory, and virtually every open-source fine-tuning pipeline. If you've fine-tuned any model on Darja data or any low-resource language, you've almost certainly used this. It's also what made DPO practically accessible — without LoRA, running preference optimization on consumer hardware would be out of reach for most researchers.

_Hu et al., 2021 — "LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models" — arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685_
