
**Adding "let's think step by step" to a prompt and watching benchmark scores jump. That's essentially what this paper discovered.**

The CoT paper (Wei et al., 2022) made a simple but profound observation: if you show a large language model examples that include intermediate reasoning steps — not just question → answer, but question → _working_ → answer — the model generalizes that pattern and starts reasoning through new problems the same way.

The results on math word problems were striking. Prompting a 540B-parameter model with just eight chain-of-thought exemplars achieved state-of-the-art accuracy on the GSM8K benchmark, surpassing even fine-tuned GPT-3 with a verifier.

But here's the catch that the paper is honest about: chain-of-thought prompting is an emergent ability of model scale — it doesn't positively impact performance for small models, and only yields gains when used with models of roughly 100B parameters. Below that threshold, models produce fluent but logically broken reasoning chains. The technique only works if the model is already large enough to "think."

The honest open question — still unresolved in 2026 — is whether the model is actually reasoning or just pattern-matching on reasoning-shaped text it saw during pretraining. The chains look like logic. Whether they _are_ logic is a different question.

**Why it matters now:** CoT is the direct ancestor of everything you see in modern reasoning models — o1, DeepSeek-R1, Gemini Thinking. They all extend this idea: if you make intermediate reasoning explicit and reward it, models get dramatically better at hard problems. This paper is where that trajectory started.
