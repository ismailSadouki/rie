

**Chain-of-thought gives a model one shot to reason correctly. Self-consistency gives it many — then takes a vote.**

That's the entire idea of this paper (Wang et al., 2022), and it's embarrassingly effective.

The problem with standard CoT: you sample one reasoning path greedily and commit to whatever answer it produces. If the model takes a wrong turn early, there's no recovery. One bad step, wrong answer.

Self-consistency's fix: instead of one greedy path, sample a diverse set of reasoning chains for the same question — different intermediate steps, different logical routes — and then pick the final answer that appears most consistently across all of them. No reranker, no verifier, no additional model. Just majority vote over multiple reasoning traces.

The gains are significant. On GSM8K (math word problems), self-consistency pushes PaLM 540B from 56.9% to 74.4% accuracy. On a range of commonsense and symbolic reasoning benchmarks, it outperforms standard CoT by substantial margins — often closing the gap with much larger models.

The honest limitation: it's expensive. You're running the model 10, 20, 40 times per question instead of once. At inference time, that cost multiplies directly with usage. For production systems where latency and compute matter, this is a real constraint — and it's why newer approaches like process reward models try to achieve similar gains more efficiently.

**Why it matters now:** Self-consistency is the conceptual ancestor of test-time compute scaling — the idea powering o1, DeepSeek-R1, and Gemini Thinking. The insight that _spending more compute at inference time improves reasoning_, rather than just training harder, is now one of the dominant research directions in 2026. This paper was an early, clean proof that the idea works.

_Wang et al., 2022 — "Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models" — arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171_
