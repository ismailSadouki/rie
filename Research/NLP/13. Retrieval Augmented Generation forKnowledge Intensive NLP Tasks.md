

**LLMs have a knowledge problem: they forget, hallucinate, and can't update what they know without retraining. RAG is the most practical fix anyone has found.**

The original RAG paper (Lewis et al., 2020) proposed something deceptively simple: instead of forcing the model to store all knowledge in its weights, let it retrieve relevant documents at inference time and condition its generation on them. Parametric memory — what the model knows — combined with non-parametric memory — what it can look up.

The architecture has three moving parts. A retriever (dense passage retrieval, not keyword search) finds relevant documents from an external corpus given the input query. Those documents get concatenated with the query and fed to the generator, which produces the final answer. The whole thing is trained end-to-end — the retriever and generator learn together.

The results on open-domain QA were strong, beating both pure parametric models and task-specific extract-and-retrieve pipelines. But the more important result is qualitative: RAG outputs are more specific, more factual, and more diverse than generation from a model relying solely on its weights.

The honest limitation the paper doesn't fully resolve: retrieval quality is a hard ceiling. If the retrieved documents are irrelevant or misleading, the model faithfully generates wrong answers. Garbage in, garbage out — but at higher confidence. This is still the central unsolved problem in RAG systems in 2026.

**Why it matters now:** RAG went from a research paper to the dominant production pattern for LLM deployment in under three years. Every enterprise chatbot, every document Q&A system, every knowledge-grounded assistant you've used runs some version of this.

_Lewis et al., 2020 — "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" — arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401_
