
**LLM inference is slow for a fundamental reason: you generate one token at a time, and each token requires a full forward pass through the entire model. Speculative decoding breaks that constraint.**

The idea in this Google paper (Leviathan et al., 2023) is elegant and counterintuitive: use a small, fast draft model to speculatively generate several tokens ahead, then verify all of them in a single parallel forward pass of the large model. If the large model agrees with the draft tokens, you've generated multiple tokens for the cost of one forward pass. If it disagrees at some point, you discard from that point onward and resume normally.

The critical guarantee — and this is what makes it non-obvious — is that the output distribution is mathematically identical to sampling from the large model alone. No approximation. No quality tradeoff. Just speed. They demonstrate 2x–3x acceleration on T5-XXL with identical outputs.

The key insight driving this: language has structure. Most tokens in a sequence are predictable — common words, continuations of phrases, syntactic patterns. A small draft model handles these easily. The large model only needs to intervene where things get genuinely hard. You're essentially routing easy tokens to cheap compute and hard tokens to expensive compute, automatically.

The honest limitation: the speedup depends entirely on how often the large model agrees with the draft model — the acceptance rate. If the draft model is too weak or the task is highly unpredictable, acceptance rates drop and the gains shrink. Choosing the right draft model for a given task is non-trivial.

**Why it matters now:** Speculative decoding is deployed in production at every major AI lab in 2026 — it's one of the primary reasons real-time LLM responses feel as fast as they do. Claude, GPT-4, and Gemini all use variants of this under the hood. It's also spawned an entire research subfield: EAGLE, Medusa, and other methods now push acceptance rates dramatically higher with smarter drafting strategies.
